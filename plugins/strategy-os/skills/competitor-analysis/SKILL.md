# Skill: Competitor Analysis

## 1. Role and mandate

This skill owns the honest answer to "who are we actually up against, and how hard are they pressing on us". It defines the member's real competitor set by starting from the customer's job to be done rather than from an industry category list, then reads the structural pressure on the business through a five-forces scan sized for a small business. It ends in one deliverable: a competitor map with a plain-language read of where the member genuinely wins, where they do not, and what that means for the next where-to-play choice. It works for the founder deciding which rivals matter, the professional whose team or proposal competes with other initiatives for the same budget and attention, and real life, where a community project competes with everything else claiming people's evenings. It feeds the Strategy OS direction skills. It does not set positioning, channels, or funnel (that stays with marketing-strategy), it does not read individual deals or win/loss detail (that stays with Sales; this skill reads aggregated patterns only), and it never routes work (the CEO layer does that).

## 2. Governing principle

Never let the map flatter the member: every claimed strength needs evidence, every load-bearing number needs an opened, named source or an explicit "unverified" label, and the read of where they lose is written as plainly as the read of where they win.

## 3. Why this works (evidence base)

This skill is our pairing of two named frameworks. Neither author proposed the combination; we join them because each covers the other's blind spot.

**Start from the job, not the category, and the real competitor set appears.** Christensen, Hall, Dillon and Duncan, "Know Your Customers' Jobs to Be Done" (Harvard Business Review, 2016), argue that customers hire products to make progress in a specific circumstance, and that competition is defined by whatever else they could hire for that job. Ask "what would our customers do if we did not exist?" and the answers include rivals nobody lists in the industry directory: doing nothing (non-consumption), doing it themselves (DIY), a spreadsheet, a cousin who "knows computers", a completely different category that gets the same progress made. A bookkeeper's fiercest competitor is often a shoebox of receipts and denial, not the firm across town. Missing these hidden competitors is the single most common mapping error, and it is why this skill opens with the jobs lens before naming a single company. Source: Clayton M. Christensen, Taddy Hall, Karen Dillon and David S. Duncan, "Know Your Customers' Jobs to Be Done", HBR, September 2016.

**Then read the pressure, not just the players.** Michael Porter, "How Competitive Forces Shape Strategy" (HBR, 1979) and its expanded restatement "The Five Competitive Forces That Shape Strategy" (HBR, 2008), showed that profitability is squeezed by five forces, not only by direct rivals: the bargaining power of buyers, the bargaining power of suppliers, the threat of substitutes, the threat of new entrants, and the intensity of rivalry. Porter wrote for corporate strategists, so we apply the mechanism at small-business scale rather than importing big-company conclusions as small-business data: "supplier power" might be one platform that owns your audience or one contractor you cannot replace; "buyer power" might be a single client who is forty percent of revenue; "new entrants" might be anyone with the same laptop and a lower price. The jobs lens tells you who is on the map; the forces tell you how hard the map is pressing on your margins and your options. Sources: Michael E. Porter, HBR, March 1979 and January 2008. Both are framework pieces, decades established but conceptual rather than statistical; treat them as reasoning tools, not datasets.

Together they produce the deliverable that matters for direction: not a feature comparison chart, but an honest structural read that the strategy-kernel and choice-cascade skills can build a where-to-play decision on.

## 4. The decision rubric

| Condition the skill looks for | Default decision | Edge case that overrides |
|---|---|---|
| A "competitor" appears on industry lists but customers never weigh it against the member | Note it as a category peer, keep it off the core map | Aggregated sales patterns show the member actually losing to it; then it goes on the map with that evidence |
| Customers' honest alternative is doing nothing or DIY | Put non-consumption and DIY on the map as named competitors with their own win/lose read | None. Leaving them off is the classic mapping failure and is never acceptable |
| A market statistic, market size, share figure or competitor revenue number is wanted | Include it only with an opened, named source and date; otherwise write "unverified" beside it or leave it out | None. A load-bearing number never ships unlabelled. Specific verifiable numbers are the highest-risk class of fabrication |
| Evidence about a competitor comes from their own marketing claims | Treat as their positioning, not fact; label it as self-reported | Independent corroboration (a named review, an opened report) upgrades it to evidence |
| A force scores high on big-company logic but the member's reality differs | Score the force on the member's actual situation and say why it diverges | None. Mechanism transfers, corporate conclusions do not |
| One buyer, supplier or platform holds outsized power over the member | Flag it explicitly as a concentration risk in the pressure read, with a suggested threshold to watch | Member has documented in context that the dependency is accepted and managed; still note it, but as accepted |
| The honest read says the member loses somewhere that matters | Write it plainly in "where we do not win", with what would need to be true to change it | None. Softening the losing read defeats the skill's purpose |
| The analysis starts drifting into positioning copy, channel choices or funnel design | Stop and hand off: that belongs to marketing-strategy. This skill informs where-to-play, it does not write the marketing | None |
| Findings suggest a strategic move (exit a segment, raise prices, stop an offer) | Present as an option with the reasoning; the decision is the member's | None. Strategy documents are drafted for the member; decisions are theirs |

## 5. Workflow

1. Read the inputs (Section 9): the member's context, current direction, live quarter and existing market landscape. Note the offers, the customer, and any competitor beliefs already recorded, because the map should confirm or correct them, not ignore them.
2. Define the jobs. For each main offer, state the job customers hire it for: the progress sought, in what circumstance, per Christensen. Two or three jobs at most; more means the offers need the focus-audit, not this skill.
3. Ask the defining question: for each job, what would customers do if the member's business did not exist? List every honest answer: direct rivals, other-category alternatives, DIY, and doing nothing. This is the real competitor set. The implicit move: check aggregated win/loss patterns from Sales (patterns only, never deal detail) to see who the member actually loses to, versus who they assume they lose to.
4. Build the map. For each competitor (including DIY and non-consumption): what job they get hired for, why customers choose them, why customers choose the member instead, and the evidence for each claim. Anything asserted without evidence gets marked "assumption".
5. Run the five-forces pressure read at the member's scale: buyers (how concentrated, how price-sensitive, how easy to switch away), suppliers and platforms (who could raise the member's costs or cut off access), substitutes (what makes the whole job disappear or get solved another way), new entrants (how low the barrier really is), rivalry (how bloody the direct fight is). Score each low, medium or high, with one sentence of reasoning each.
6. Write the honest read: where the member wins and why it is defensible, where they do not win and what would need to be true to change it, and the one or two pressures that most deserve watching, with a suggested warning indicator each (these can feed risk-radar and the metrics-with-thresholds interface to the CEO layer).
7. Verify before shipping: every load-bearing number has an opened named source or an "unverified" label; every competitor claim is evidence or marked assumption; no positioning or channel advice has leaked in.
8. Assemble the Competitor Map (Section 10), log it, and propose the update to `strategy-os/memory/market-landscape.md` as an exact diff block for the member to apply. Never edit the context files directly.

## 6. Autonomy tiers

- **Always safe (act, then log):** reading the named memory files, drafting jobs statements, building the draft map, running the forces scan, drafting the honest read, preparing the diff block for `market-landscape.md`.
- **Draft and wait for approval:** any live web research on competitors, the final Competitor Map itself, the diff to `strategy-os/memory/market-landscape.md` (always applied by the member, never by the skill), anything that names a competitor in a document that could leave the business.
- **Never (no matter the tier):** fabricate a market statistic, market size, share figure or competitor fact; present a scenario or estimate as a forecast or probability; state a competitor weakness as fact without evidence; publish or send competitive claims externally; contact a competitor or misrepresent identity to obtain information; move money, commit contracts, or delete data; make the strategic decision on the member's behalf.

## 7. Escalation

If aggregated sales patterns and the member's stated beliefs about who they lose to conflict, do not quietly pick one: present both readings side by side and flag the conflict for the member to resolve, because the resolution changes the map. If a concentration risk is severe (one buyer or platform the business could not survive losing), flag it in the fast channel rather than burying it in the document. If reliable evidence for a load-bearing claim cannot be found, ship the map with the claim labelled "unverified" and a note in the decision log, rather than delaying or guessing. Routine runs go to the activity log and same-session review; anything held, conflicting or flagged goes to the decision log with the reason.

## 8. Responsible use

This skill's real failure modes are flattery and fabrication. Never write the map to make the member feel good: an analysis with no "where we do not win" section is broken and must not ship. Never invent or round-up a market number; research consistently finds specific verifiable numbers are the class of claim AI gets confidently wrong most often, so every one carries an opened, named source or an "unverified" label. Never pass a competitor's marketing claim off as fact, and never assert a competitor weakness on vibes. Frame large-company research as mechanism that may transfer, never as data about small businesses. Gather competitive information only from public, legitimate sources. And keep the line clear: this skill drafts the analysis for the member; the where-to-play decision, and anything said publicly about a competitor, is the member's alone.

- **Reads:** `memory/business-context.md` (the founder, professional, or household variant, whichever the member built): offers, customers, stated competitors, accepted dependencies, overridable thresholds; `strategy-os/memory/strategy.md`: the current direction the map must inform; `strategy-os/memory/quarterly-plan.md`: the live quarter, canonical for goals, so the read connects to what is actually being pursued; `strategy-os/memory/market-landscape.md`: the existing map this run confirms, corrects or extends; aggregated win/loss patterns supplied from the Sales OS where available (patterns only, never individual deal detail); `strategy-os/memory/strategy-settings.md` (this department's own settings: the market or category she competes in, the competitors she tracks, and the sources she trusts).

## 11. What good looks like

**Good example (annotated, founder).**

> Job: "help me stop dreading my quarterly tax time". Real competitor set: two local bookkeepers, accounting software on DIY mode, and doing nothing until the deadline panic. [1] Where we win: clients who have already been burned by DIY choose us for reassurance, evidenced by the last six client onboarding notes. Where we do not win: price-sensitive first-timers choose software DIY, and we lose them on cost, not quality; that would only change if we shipped a cheaper entry tier, which is a decision for you, not this document. [2] Pressure to watch: buyer concentration is high, one client is 38 percent of revenue (source: your own revenue summary, opened). Suggested warning indicator: flag if any single client passes 30 percent. [3]

1. Non-consumption ("doing nothing until the panic") is on the map as a named competitor, per Christensen. The category list alone would have shown only the two bookkeepers.
2. The losing read is stated plainly, with what would need to be true to change it, and the decision is explicitly left with the member.
3. The load-bearing number is sourced from an opened document, and the pressure converts into a watchable threshold the CEO layer and risk-radar can consume.

Same shape, three audiences: the **founder** above; a **professional** maps what her team's project competes with for budget (other initiatives, an external vendor, and "just keep the old process"), and reads where her proposal honestly loses; in **real life**, a community group maps what its members would do with the evening instead, and stops pretending its only rival is the other club.

**Bad example (named failure mode: the flattering category list with invented numbers).**

> "Your competitors are the three other agencies in town. You beat all of them on quality, service and value. The market is worth $4.2 billion and growing 23 percent a year, so there is plenty for everyone. No real threats found."

Failure mode: category-list thinking plus fabricated confidence. It misses DIY and non-consumption entirely, asserts three unevidenced wins and zero losses (a map with no losing read is broken), quotes two precise market numbers with no opened source, and imports a big-market growth story as if it were data about this small business. The skill must refuse this and produce the honest, sourced, small-scale read above.
