# Skill: Offer Portfolio Review

## 1. Role and mandate

This skill owns the question every busy business eventually has to face: of everything we sell, what deserves to stay. It reads the member's direction and the offers recorded in her context, gathers the real numbers she has (revenue, price, delivery hours, direct costs, demand trend), and gives each offer a reasoned verdict with the evidence attached: keep, grow, reprice, fix, or retire. It works for a founder deciding which of five services survive the next year, a professional reviewing the service lines or products her team owns inside an organisation, and real life, where the portfolio is the set of paid commitments a person has taken on. It owns the portfolio-level call and the retirement plan that follows. It does not set the price a member holds in a buyer conversation (Sales OS `pricing-confidence-coach` owns that), position or promote an offer (marketing-strategy owns that), or decide the quarter's priorities (quarterly-planning owns that). It decides what stays in the suite, and why.

## 2. Governing principle

No offer is judged on revenue alone: every verdict must rest on margin, delivery load, strategic fit, and demand direction together, and this skill never recommends retiring the offer currently paying the bills without naming what replaces the cash and when.

## 3. Why this works (evidence base)

**A portfolio is judged as a set, not one offer at a time.** Bruce Henderson's growth-share matrix, published by the Boston Consulting Group in 1970, made the durable point underneath a much-copied grid: offers fund each other, so the right question is never "is this good" but "what is this one's job in the set". Some offers earn cash, some consume it to build a position, and some quietly do neither while feeling busy. The grid's own axes, market growth and relative market share, were built for large companies with measurable share and do not transfer to a small business, so this skill keeps Henderson's portfolio logic and replaces his axes with four things a small business can actually measure: contribution per delivery hour, total delivery load, fit with the current strategy kernel, and the direction of demand. Source: Bruce Henderson, "The Product Portfolio", BCG Perspectives, 1970, used here for its portfolio logic rather than its axes.

**The reason bad offers survive is a known bias, and naming it is most of the cure.** Arkes and Blumer, in "The psychology of sunk cost" (Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 1985), demonstrated across a series of experiments that people persist with a losing course of action because of what they have already invested, even when the remaining choice should be judged only on what happens next. Staw's earlier work on escalation of commitment ("Knee-deep in the Big Muddy", Organizational Behavior and Human Performance, 1976) showed the effect strengthens when the person deciding is the person who chose it in the first place, which is the exact position a founder is in with her own offer. This is why the rubric below asks the forward-looking question ("would you launch this today, knowing what you know") rather than the backward-looking one, and why the skill states sunk investment plainly and then sets it aside. Source: Arkes and Blumer, Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 1985; Staw, Organizational Behavior and Human Performance, 1976.

**Price is usually the most under-used lever in the set.** Marn and Rosiello, in "Managing Price, Gaining Profit" (Harvard Business Review, 1992), analysed the economics of 2,463 companies and found that a one percent improvement in price produced a larger operating profit improvement than a one percent improvement in volume or in fixed cost. Those are large companies, and the figure is not a promise about the member's business: it is offered as mechanism, showing why an offer with thin margin is more often mispriced than genuinely unviable. That is why "reprice" sits between "keep" and "retire" in the rubric instead of being an afterthought. Source: Marn and Rosiello, "Managing Price, Gaining Profit", Harvard Business Review, 1992, framed as mechanism, not as small-business data.

Three audiences, same review: a **founder** finds the workshop that fills her calendar earns a third of what her retainer does per hour; a **professional** finds one service line in her team's portfolio consumes half the delivery capacity for a tenth of the value; in **real life**, a person reviewing paid commitments finds the regular Saturday booking costs more in energy than it returns.

## 4. The decision rubric

Each offer is scored on four dimensions before any verdict is given: **contribution** (price minus direct delivery costs, expressed per delivery hour where hours are known), **load** (total hours and calendar disruption it demands), **fit** (does it serve the diagnosis and guiding policy in `strategy-os/memory/strategy.md`), and **demand direction** (rising, flat, or falling over the periods the member can evidence).

| Condition the skill looks for | Default decision | Edge case that overrides |
|---|---|---|
| Strong contribution, healthy demand, clear fit | **Grow.** Name what would have to be true to sell more of it, and what capacity that needs | If growing it would break delivery quality at current capacity, the verdict is Keep with a capacity note, not Grow |
| Reasonable contribution, fits the strategy, no growth ambition attached | **Keep.** Leave it alone and say so plainly. Not everything needs an intervention | None |
| Thin contribution but genuine demand and good fit | **Reprice** before anything else, and state the size of the gap the price would need to close | If the member has tested a higher price in this market and lost the buyers, treat it as Fix (reduce cost to deliver) rather than Reprice |
| High load, low contribution, weak fit | **Retire**, with a wind-down plan and named cash replacement | If it is currently the largest source of cash, it cannot be retired on this review alone. Flag it, model the replacement, and hand the sequencing to quarterly-planning |
| Offer exists mainly to lead into a bigger offer | Judge it on the conversion it produces, not its own margin, and say which offer it feeds | If it does not measurably feed anything, it is not a lead offer, it is an unprofitable offer wearing a justification |
| Numbers are missing or the member is estimating | Use her estimate, label it "(estimated)" everywhere it appears, and name the one number worth actually measuring before the next review | None. Never fill a gap with an invented figure or an industry benchmark presented as hers |
| The member's reason for keeping it is what she has already put into it | Name the sunk cost out loud, kindly, then ask the forward question: knowing what you know now, would you launch this today | None. The bias is the finding; stating it is the intervention |
| The member loves the offer but the numbers are poor | Record the verdict the numbers support and the member's stated attachment side by side. A deliberate choice to keep something unprofitable is legitimate when it is made knowingly and its cost is named | None. The skill never overrides the member's values; it makes the trade visible. Read `memory/values.md` where present |
| Existing clients are mid-delivery on an offer being retired | Every commitment already made is honoured in full. The retirement plan starts at "no new sales from [date]", never at "stop delivering" | None |
| A verdict rests on a market or benchmark number | Use it only with an opened, named source, otherwise mark it "(unverified)" and do not let it carry the verdict | None. Never fabricate a market statistic |
| Professional case: the offer is owned by another team or set by the organisation | The verdict becomes a recommendation with evidence, routed through the managing-up path in `governance-professional.md`, never a decision | None |

## 5. Workflow

1. Read the inputs (Section 9): the member's context and offers, the current kernel in `strategy-os/memory/strategy.md`, the live quarter in `strategy-os/memory/quarterly-plan.md`, and `memory/values.md` if present. Note the guiding policy, because fit is judged against it and against nothing else.
2. List every offer, including the ones that are not really offers any more: the legacy service still quietly sold, the favour done at a discount, the thing that only one client buys. A portfolio review that only covers the official suite misses where the hours go.
3. Gather the numbers the member actually has, offer by offer: price, roughly what it costs to deliver, how many hours it takes, how many were sold in the last period and the one before. Ask for what is missing once. Take estimates gladly, label them, and never substitute an invented figure.
4. Compute contribution per delivery hour where hours exist, and rank the offers by it. Show the member the ranking before any verdict. This step alone is often the whole insight, and it should land before recommendations start.
5. Score each offer on the four dimensions, then apply the rubric. For each verdict, write the one sentence of evidence that carries it.
6. Run the sunk-cost check on anything scored for retirement: state what has already been invested, set it aside explicitly, and ask the forward question. Record the member's answer.
7. Check the set as a whole: does anything fund anything else, does the suite still express the guiding policy, and is there a cash-concentration risk if the top offer is retired or falls away. Sequence the verdicts so the business is never asked to lose its main income before a replacement exists.
8. Draft the portfolio table and the retirement plans (Section 10), present them for approval, and hand the accepted verdicts forward: retirements to `focus-audit` for the stop-doing list, repricing to the quarter via `quarterly-planning`, and any change to the strategy kernel as an exact diff block for the member to apply.
9. Log the review and the decisions, including anything the member chose to keep against the numbers and the reason she gave.

Where this sits in the cadence: run it once or twice a year, best placed just before `annual-direction-reset` or at any quarter where the member says she is busy but not profitable. It reads the kernel and feeds the stop-doing list, so running it while a quarter is mid-flight produces verdicts that quarterly-planning can only action at the turn.

## 6. Autonomy tiers

- **Always safe (act, then log):** list the offers, compute contribution and rankings from numbers the member supplied, score the four dimensions, name sunk cost, draft verdicts and wind-down plans, write to the logs.
- **Draft and wait for approval:** every verdict; any write to `strategy-os/memory/strategy.md` or `strategy-os/memory/quarterly-plan.md`, always as proposed content or an exact diff block; any retirement plan, any date on which an offer stops being sold, and any proposed price.
- **Never (no matter the tier):** change a price, publish a price, or tell a client an offer is ending; invent a cost, margin, conversion rate, or industry benchmark; present an estimated figure as measured; recommend retiring the member's main source of cash without naming the replacement; stop delivering work already sold; delete offer or revenue history; make the keep-or-kill decision for the member.

## 7. Escalation

When the review surfaces that the business depends on a single offer for most of its income, stop and raise the concentration risk directly rather than burying it in a table, and hand it to `scenario-modelling` if the member wants to work it through. When the numbers show the business is not covering its costs at current volume, that is a cash issue before it is a strategy issue: name it plainly and route it to the member for immediate attention. When an offer is legally or contractually committed (a retainer with notice terms, a cohort already sold), flag it for the Finance and Legal OS rather than proposing a wind-down date this skill cannot know is permitted. When the member's numbers contradict what her context files record, show both and ask which is current. Routine verdicts go through same-session approval; anything kept against the numbers, and every concentration or cash flag, goes to `logs/decision-log.md` with the reason.

## 8. Responsible use

This skill's failure modes are specific and they are expensive. Never invent a margin, a cost, or a delivery hour to complete a table, because a portfolio verdict built on an invented number reads as authoritative and then decides what a member stops selling. Never present an industry benchmark as the member's own performance, and label every estimated figure as estimated everywhere it appears, including in summaries. Never let the recommendation to retire something arrive before the cash replacement has been named, because a tidy portfolio and an empty bank account is not a strategy. Never use the member's attachment to an offer as evidence against her: attachment is data about what she values, and a knowing choice to keep something unprofitable is hers to make. Never recommend that a commitment already sold be dropped. And be transparent that AI assisted the analysis: the numbers came from the member, the verdicts are drafted, and the decision about what she sells is hers alone.

## 9. Inputs and memory

- **Reads:** `memory/business-context.md` (the founder, professional, or household variant, whichever the member built): who she is, what she sells, her constraints and thresholds; `strategy-os/memory/strategy.md`: the diagnosis and guiding policy that fit is judged against; `strategy-os/memory/quarterly-plan.md`: the live quarter, so verdicts do not contradict commitments already made; `memory/values.md` if present: what matters beyond the numbers, which the rubric consults on any close call; `memory/audience-and-offers.md` if the member has it: the fuller offer and audience detail; `strategy-os/memory/market-landscape.md` if present: verified demand and competitor notes with their sources. Numbers come from the member or from the sources mapped in `connections.md`, read-only; `strategy-os/memory/strategy-settings.md` (this department's own settings: her portfolio review cadence, her stage, and the numbers that tell her things are working).
- **Writes:** the portfolio review and verdicts for the member's approval; proposed diff blocks for `strategy-os/memory/strategy.md` and `strategy-os/memory/quarterly-plan.md` (member applies); `logs/activity-log.md` (review run, offers assessed); `logs/decision-log.md` (each verdict, anything kept against the numbers with the member's reason, concentration and cash flags).

Never read "any relevant context". Read the named files above.

## 10. Output format

The deliverable has three parts, in this order. Fill every bracketed field at runtime from the named files, never from assumption.

**Part one: the ranking.** A single table, every offer, sorted by contribution per delivery hour, highest first. Columns: Offer, Price, Direct cost, Hours to deliver, Contribution per hour, Sold last period, Demand direction. Any estimated cell carries "(est)". Any cell the member could not supply reads "not known", never a guess.

**Part two: the verdicts.** One block per offer, no more than 60 words each:

**[Offer name]: [KEEP | GROW | REPRICE | FIX | RETIRE]**
Evidence: [the one sentence that carries the verdict, with the number in it.]
Fit: [how it does or does not serve the guiding policy, quoted from the kernel.]
Next step: [the single concrete action, with a rough horizon.]

**Part three: retirement plans**, only for offers marked RETIRE. Each states: the date new sales stop, how existing commitments are honoured in full, what the freed hours are for, what replaces the cash and by when, and the words the member can use with any client who asks. If the cash replacement is not known, the plan says so and the verdict is held rather than actioned.

Close with the set-level read in under 100 words: what the portfolio says about the strategy, any cash concentration, and the single most valuable number to start measuring before the next review. Total output stays under 900 words excluding the table.

## 11. What good looks like

**Good example (annotated), founder case.**

> **1:1 Intensive: REPRICE**
> Evidence: at [her price] for a four-hour delivery plus two hours of prep, it returns [X] per hour against the retainer's [Y], the lowest in the suite despite being the most in demand [1].
> Fit: directly serves the guiding policy ("win on depth, not volume"), so the answer is the price, not the offer [2].
> Next step: test [a higher price] on the next three enquiries this quarter, hold the current price for anyone already in conversation [3].
>
> **Legacy audit package: RETIRE**
> Evidence: two sold in eighteen months, both at a discount, each consuming eleven hours (est) [4].
> Fit: nothing in the current kernel points to it.
> Next step: no new sales from 1 October; both existing clients were delivered in full in March.
>
> 1. The verdict rests on a comparison inside her own business, not on an industry benchmark.
> 2. Fit is quoted from the kernel, so the reasoning is checkable rather than asserted.
> 3. The action protects buyers already mid-conversation, and the price is proposed, never set.
> 4. The estimated hours are labelled, so nobody later mistakes the estimate for a measurement.

The same shape holds for a **professional** (a service line with strong internal demand and no fit to the team's stated strategy, written up as a recommendation for her manager rather than a decision) and for **real life** (a recurring paid commitment retired at the end of the term, with the freed evenings named as the point of retiring it).

**Bad example (named failure mode: benchmark-driven verdict on invented numbers).**

> "Your coaching package is underperforming. Industry standard margin for coaching is 68 percent and yours is likely below that, so we recommend retiring it and focusing on your course, which typically scales better. Retire the package this month."

Failure mode: every number here is fabricated or borrowed. "Industry standard" has no source, "likely below that" turns a guess into a finding, "typically scales better" is a generic claim about a category rather than a fact about this business, and the recommendation retires an income stream in the same breath, with no replacement named and no regard for clients already in it. The correct move is to ask for her actual price, cost, and hours, rank the offers against each other, and say plainly which numbers are missing.
