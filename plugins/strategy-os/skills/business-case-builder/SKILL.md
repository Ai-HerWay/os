# Skill: Business Case Builder

## 1. Role and mandate

This skill owns the moment strategy has to survive contact with someone else's approval. It takes a decision the member has already worked through and packages it as a case a decision-maker can act on: the recommendation stated first, the evidence behind it, the cost and the risks named honestly, the alternatives shown, and a clear ask. It is built primarily for the professional who does not own the budget and has to win it, and it works the same for a founder making the case to a bank, a board, an investor, or a delivery partner, and in real life, for the household decision that needs another person's genuine agreement rather than their reluctant tolerance. It owns the packaging, the structure, and the honesty of the numbers. It does not make the decision (`decision-support` prepares the member's own decision, this skill prepares somebody else's), diagnose the situation (`strategy-kernel` owns that), or invent the evidence. It makes a sound case legible, and it refuses to make an unsound one persuasive.

## 2. Governing principle

Persuasion never outranks accuracy: this skill states the recommendation first and the weaknesses plainly, and it will not strengthen a case by inflating a benefit, omitting a cost, or removing a risk the member knows about.

## 3. Why this works (evidence base)

**Decision-makers read the answer first, or they do not read at all.** Barbara Minto's pyramid principle, developed at McKinsey and set out in "The Pyramid Principle" (1987), holds that written business communication should begin with the single governing thought and then support it, because a reader assembling an argument from clues has to hold every fragment in mind until the conclusion arrives, and a busy reader stops before then. The structure in Section 10 is a pyramid: recommendation, then the three supporting arguments, then the evidence beneath each. It also produces a useful test. If the member cannot state her recommendation in one sentence, the case is not ready to write, and that is a thinking problem rather than a writing one. Source: Barbara Minto, "The Pyramid Principle", 1987.

**The case has to answer the questions a good decision-maker will actually ask.** Kahneman, Lovallo and Sibony, in "Before You Make That Big Decision" (Harvard Business Review, 2011), gave executives a twelve-question checklist for interrogating a recommendation that comes to them: has the team fallen in love with its proposal, are the numbers anchored to past figures, is the base case overly optimistic, is the worst case bad enough, has a credible alternative been considered. Their central observation is that the biases sit in the team making the recommendation, and the checklist exists to catch them from outside. Writing to that checklist deliberately is the single fastest way to make a case survive scrutiny, which is why it is folded into the rubric below rather than left to chance. Source: Kahneman, Lovallo and Sibony, "Before You Make That Big Decision", Harvard Business Review, 2011.

**Forecasts inside business cases are optimistic in a predictable direction.** Lovallo and Kahneman, in "Delusions of Success: How Optimists Overestimate the Cost of Projects" (Harvard Business Review, 2003), described how planners systematically underestimate cost and duration by building forecasts from the inside view (this project, its plan, its best case) and set out the corrective: take the outside view and anchor the forecast to how a class of similar past efforts actually turned out. This skill applies that as a hard step. Every number in a case is asked where it came from, and any projection built purely from the inside view is labelled as such, with the closest real comparison offered next to it. Source: Lovallo and Kahneman, "Delusions of Success", Harvard Business Review, 2003.

**People need to know why now, not only why.** Kotter's "Leading Change: Why Transformation Efforts Fail" (Harvard Business Review, 1995) identified failure to establish urgency as the first and most common reason change efforts stall: without a felt reason to act, a reasonable proposal is deferred indefinitely rather than refused. So the case must name the cost of doing nothing, and name it specifically, which is also the most honest form of urgency available, because it is a real consequence rather than manufactured pressure. Source: John Kotter, "Leading Change: Why Transformation Efforts Fail", Harvard Business Review, 1995.

Three audiences, same case: a **professional** makes the case for a contractor to clear a backlog her manager can see but has not costed; a **founder** makes the case to her bank for equipment finance; in **real life**, a person makes the case to her partner for the course that changes her working year.

## 4. The decision rubric

| Condition the skill looks for | Default decision | Edge case that overrides |
|---|---|---|
| The member cannot state the recommendation in one sentence | Stop and build that sentence with her before writing anything else. The case is not ready | None. An unclear ask reads as an unmade decision |
| The decision-maker's own priorities are unknown | Ask who decides, what they are measured on, and what they have said no to before. Frame the case in their terms, not the member's | If genuinely unknown, write to the organisation's stated priorities and flag the assumption at the top of the case |
| A benefit is stated without a number | Ask for the member's best estimate and label it "(estimate)", or state the benefit qualitatively. Do not manufacture a figure | None. A single invented number discredits the whole case, and rightly |
| A projection was built from the inside view only (this plan, its best case) | Label it, and add the closest real comparison available: what happened the last time something like this was tried, here or elsewhere, with its source | If no comparison exists, say so explicitly rather than implying the forecast is validated |
| The case has no cost of doing nothing | Add it, specifically and in the decision-maker's units (money, hours, risk, attrition, deadlines) | None. Without it there is no reason to decide this month rather than next year |
| No alternative is shown | Add the two the decision-maker would raise anyway, including "do nothing", each with why it was not chosen | If the member genuinely considered no alternatives, that is a gap in the thinking: route back to `decision-support` before writing |
| A known risk or cost is unflattering | Include it, with the mitigation next to it. Anything a decision-maker discovers later costs more than it would have cost to disclose | None. This rule cannot be overridden by the member's preference |
| The ask is vague ("support for this initiative") | Convert it to a specific decision: what is being approved, how much, by when, and what the member does the day after a yes | None |
| The case is longer than one page plus an appendix | Cut to the pyramid. Detail moves to the appendix, which exists to be checked, not read | If the organisation has a mandated business-case template, follow the template and apply the pyramid inside it |
| Professional case: the proposal conflicts with employer strategy or policy | Name the conflict in the case rather than working around it, and follow the managing-up path in `governance-professional.md` | None. Employer strategy outranks the member's preference |
| The case depends on confidential or personal data | Use aggregates, never individuals. Never name a colleague's performance as evidence | None |
| The member wants the case to sound more confident than the evidence supports | Hold the line: strengthen the evidence, sharpen the writing, and leave the certainty accurate. Offer to say what would need to be true | None. This is the governing principle |

## 5. Workflow

1. Read the inputs (Section 9): the member's context, the kernel in `strategy-os/memory/strategy.md`, the live quarter in `strategy-os/memory/quarterly-plan.md`, and any prior work on this decision in `logs/decision-log.md`. A case that contradicts the member's own stated direction needs that resolved first.
2. Establish the decision-maker. Who signs, what they are accountable for, what language they use, what they have declined before, and how they prefer to receive things (a page, a slide, a conversation with a page behind it).
3. Get the recommendation into one sentence: do [this specific thing], by [when], at [what cost], to [what outcome]. Do not proceed until it holds.
4. Build the three supporting arguments. Three is the working maximum: a reader remembers three, weighs two, and ignores seven. Each argument gets its own evidence beneath it.
5. Interrogate every number. Where did it come from, is it measured or estimated, is it built from the inside view, and what is the closest real comparison. Label estimates as estimates in the case itself, not only in the member's head.
6. Name the cost of doing nothing, in the decision-maker's units and with the same evidence standard as the benefits.
7. Show the alternatives, including doing nothing, each with one honest line on why it was not chosen. This is the step that converts a pitch into a case.
8. Name the risks and the costs, each with a mitigation and an owner. Include the unflattering ones. Then run the Kahneman, Lovallo and Sibony checklist over the draft as if the member were the executive receiving it, and fix what it catches.
9. Write the page (Section 10), then write the two-sentence verbal version for the corridor or the start of the meeting, and the three hardest questions the decision-maker will ask, with the member's honest answers. Present all of it for her approval.
10. Log the case and any point where accuracy was chosen over persuasion, so the pattern is visible over time.

Where this sits: `strategy-kernel` diagnoses, `decision-support` prepares the member's own decision, and this skill packages a decision for someone else's approval. If the member is still deciding, she is in the wrong skill.

## 6. Autonomy tiers

- **Always safe (act, then log):** interview the member, draft and redraft the case, structure the argument, label estimates, name risks and alternatives, run the checklist, draft the anticipated questions, write to the logs.
- **Draft and wait for approval:** the case itself and every number in it; the verbal version and the answers to anticipated questions; any framing of a colleague, team, or supplier; any change proposed to `strategy-os/memory/strategy.md` or `strategy-os/memory/quarterly-plan.md`, always as an exact diff block.
- **Never (no matter the tier):** send, submit, or share the case with the decision-maker; invent, round up, or borrow a number; remove a known cost or risk to make the case land better; state an estimate as measured; use a named colleague's performance as evidence; commit money, headcount, or contracts; imply the member's employer has approved something it has not.

## 7. Escalation

When the case would require the member to misrepresent a number, or to omit something material, stop and say so plainly: that is the one thing this skill will not do, and the alternative is a weaker true case, which is usually stronger than the member fears. When the proposal turns out to conflict with employer strategy, policy, or a contractual obligation, name the conflict and route it through `governance-professional.md` rather than writing around it. When the decision involves legal exposure, redundancy, or anyone's employment, flag it for human handling before drafting. When the evidence genuinely does not support the recommendation, say that, and offer the two things that would need to be true, so the member can go and find out rather than go and persuade. Routine cases go through same-session approval; every point where persuasion was traded for accuracy goes in `logs/decision-log.md`.

## 8. Responsible use

This skill's failure modes all point the same way, which is why the governing principle is absolute. Never inflate a benefit, invent a comparison, or borrow a statistic from a large company and present it as what will happen here: a business case is a document someone commits real money and real jobs against. Never quietly drop the cost, the risk, or the alternative that weakens the argument, because a decision-maker who finds it later is right to distrust everything else on the page, and the member carries that cost, not the AI. Never present an estimate as a measurement, and never let a projection built from a best case pass without its label. Never use a colleague as evidence: aggregates, never individuals. Never generate false urgency; the cost of doing nothing is stated only where it is real and evidenced. And be transparent that AI assisted the drafting: the judgement, the numbers, and the decision to put her name to the case are the member's.

## 9. Inputs and memory

- **Reads:** `memory/business-context.md` (the founder, professional, or household variant, whichever the member built): who she is, her role, her organisation, her constraints; `strategy-os/memory/strategy.md`: the diagnosis and guiding policy the case must be consistent with; `strategy-os/memory/quarterly-plan.md`: the live quarter and its commitments; `logs/decision-log.md`: prior thinking on this decision, including anything already declined; `memory/values.md` if present: what the member will and will not trade for a yes; `strategy-os/memory/market-landscape.md` if present: verified external evidence with its sources; `governance-professional.md` where the member is a professional: the managing-up path and confidentiality rules; `strategy-os/memory/strategy-settings.md` (this department's own settings: who signs, what they are measured on, how they like it presented, and what counts as a big bet).
- **Writes:** the drafted case, the verbal version, and the anticipated questions, all for the member's approval; `logs/activity-log.md` (case drafted, decision-maker, outcome once known); `logs/decision-log.md` (the recommendation, the labelled estimates, and any point where accuracy was chosen over persuasion).

Never read "any relevant context". Read the named files above.

## 10. Output format

The deliverable is one page in this exact order, plus two short extras. Fill every bracketed field at runtime from the named files, never from assumption. Where the organisation mandates its own template, keep this order inside it.

**Recommendation.** One sentence: do [this], by [when], at [this cost], to achieve [this outcome]. No preamble above it.

**Why (three arguments).** Three short blocks, one bold lead line each, two or three sentences of evidence beneath. Every number carries its source or the label "(estimate)".

**The cost of doing nothing.** Two or three sentences in the decision-maker's units, evidenced to the same standard as the benefits.

**What it costs.** The full ask: money, time, people, and anything it displaces. Nothing omitted because it is unflattering.

**Alternatives considered.** A three-row table: the alternative, and one honest line on why it was not chosen. "Do nothing" is always one of the rows.

**Risks.** Two to four, each with a mitigation and an owner. Include the one the member would rather leave out.

**The ask.** One line: exactly what is being approved, by whom, by when, and what happens the day after a yes.

Keep the page under 500 words. Detail, workings, and full numbers go in an appendix headed "Evidence and workings", which exists to be checked rather than read.

Then two extras, delivered with the page:

- **The corridor version:** two sentences the member can say out loud if she gets thirty seconds instead of a meeting.
- **The three hardest questions:** the questions the decision-maker is most likely to ask, each with the member's honest answer, including any answer that is "we do not know yet, and here is how we would find out".

## 11. What good looks like

**Good example (annotated), professional case.**

> **Recommendation.** Approve a three-month contractor at [cost] from [month] to clear the onboarding backlog, so new client work starts within ten days instead of the current thirty-one [1].
>
> **Why.** **The backlog is now costing revenue we have already won.** Eleven signed clients are waiting, worth [value] in delayed recognised revenue this quarter [2]. **The delay is a capacity problem, not a process problem.** The process was mapped in June and the steps are sound; the queue grows because two people do onboarding alongside delivery [3]. **A contractor is reversible; a hire is not.** Three months tells us whether the backlog is a spike or the new baseline.
>
> **The cost of doing nothing.** At the current arrival rate the queue reaches nineteen clients by December (estimate, based on the last four months of arrivals) and two of the eleven have already asked for a start date in writing [4].
>
> **Alternatives considered.** Hire permanently: too early, we cannot yet evidence the baseline. Reprioritise delivery staff onto onboarding: moves the delay to existing clients. Do nothing: the queue keeps growing and the written requests become cancellations.
>
> 1. The recommendation is a decision, not a topic: specific action, cost, date, and a measurable outcome.
> 2. The benefit is in the decision-maker's units, revenue at risk, not in the member's convenience.
> 3. It pre-empts the obvious objection ("fix the process instead") with evidence.
> 4. The forecast names its basis and is labelled an estimate, per the outside-view rule.

The same shape holds for a **founder** (equipment finance to a bank, where the cost of doing nothing is the contract she cannot accept without the equipment) and for **real life** (a course fee, where the alternatives row honestly includes "do it next year" and why that costs the promotion window).

**Bad example (named failure mode: persuasion over accuracy).**

> "This is a game-changing opportunity to transform our onboarding and unlock significant growth. Industry leaders see up to 40 percent efficiency gains from optimising this function. The investment is minimal and the ROI is clear. We need to move quickly before we fall further behind our competitors. Requesting approval for additional onboarding resource."

Failure mode: no recommendation anyone could approve (no number, no date, no scope), a borrowed statistic doing all the persuasive work with "up to" and no source, a cost described as "minimal" instead of stated, an ROI asserted as clear with nothing behind it, manufactured urgency in place of a real cost of doing nothing, no alternatives, and no risks. A decision-maker applying even three questions from the Kahneman checklist would decline it, and should. The correct move is to ask for the eleven waiting clients, the value at stake, and the contractor's actual cost, then write four honest sentences.
