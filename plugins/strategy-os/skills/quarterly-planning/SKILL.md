# Skill: Quarterly Planning

## 1. Role and mandate

This skill owns the start-of-quarter ritual: turning direction into a short, dated, owned list. It reads the standing direction in `strategy-os/memory/strategy.md`, last quarter's plan and result, and the current market picture, then works with the member to choose the quarter's priorities, up to the cap she set in `strategy-os/memory/strategy-settings.md` and never more than 7. Each priority gets an owner, an outcome measure with a warning threshold, and a deadline. The finished plan is drafted into `strategy-os/memory/quarterly-plan.md`, the canonical home of goals, in the shape the CEO layer will later delegate against: one named primary objective, the priorities, per-department goal lines, declared launch windows, and the stop-doing list. It works for the founder deciding what the business does next quarter, the professional shaping her team's or her own 90-day plan inside a larger organisation, and real life, where an optional household lens plans a family quarter. It chooses WHAT happens this quarter; it never plans HOW a launch runs (campaign skills own that) and it never routes work to anyone (the CEO layer owns that).

## 2. Governing principle

The quarter holds no more than the cap the member set in `strategy-os/memory/strategy-settings.md`, and never more than seven whatever that file says. Every priority carries an owner, an outcome measure, and a deadline, and the plan is drafted for the member to decide on: the skill never sets a goal, only proposes one.

## 3. Why this works (evidence base)

**The 90-day unit is documented practitioner practice, not a productivity fad.** Gino Wickman's "Traction" (2011) codified the pattern this skill implements: each quarter, the business chooses a small number of most-important priorities, each with one owner and a due date, and everything else waits. Ninety days is long enough to finish something real and short enough that the world has not changed underneath you. Wickman's system uses its own trademarked naming; this skill implements the pattern with plain naming (quarterly priorities, quarterly plan) and credits Wickman as the source. The hard cap comes straight from the same practice: a plan with twelve priorities is a wish list wearing a plan's clothes, because attention, not ambition, is the scarce input. Source: Gino Wickman, "Traction: Get a Grip on Your Business", 2011.

**Measures must be outcomes, and the failure mode is well documented.** John Doerr's "Measure What Matters" (2018) makes the core case for outcome-based key results: a goal is only real when its measure describes a changed state of the world, not a volume of effort. The honest caveat, documented by Doerr himself and sharpened in Christina Wodtke's "Radical Focus" (2016), is that this system is widely cargo-culted: teams adopt the format, then fill it with activity ("send 20 emails", "run 6 workshops") and call it done when the effort is spent, whether or not anything moved. Wodtke's correction is the one this skill enforces: a key result is something you can fail at even after doing all the work. That is why the linter in Section 5 exists, and why it asks of every measure: "if you did all of this and nothing changed, would you call the quarter a success?" Sources: John Doerr, "Measure What Matters", 2018; Christina Wodtke, "Radical Focus", 2016.

Three audiences, same evidence: a **founder** caps the business at a handful of owned, dated outcomes; a **professional** writes a 90-day plan her manager can read in two minutes, with measures that survive the "so what changed?" question; in **real life**, the household picks one shared priority and one per person, because families burn out on twelve-item resolution lists exactly the way businesses do.

## 4. The decision rubric

| Condition the skill looks for | Default decision | Edge case that overrides |
|---|---|---|
| Candidate list holds more than her cap | Refuse to write the plan. Force-rank the list with the member and move the rest to "next quarter" or the stop-doing list. The cap never stretches | None. Her cap is the working limit and 7 is the absolute ceiling. If everything is a priority, nothing is |
| Her cap in `strategy-settings.md` is above 7 | Say so plainly and ask her to choose a number at or below 7 before planning. Do not silently plan to 7 | None. A quarter with nine priorities has none |
| `strategy-settings.md` is missing or the cap is unset | Say the file was not found rather than guessing, and plan to a default of 5 for this session | If she names a cap in the session, use it and offer to record it in the settings file |
| Fewer than 3 priorities proposed | Ask whether that is deliberate focus or avoidance; 1 or 2 is fine if chosen knowingly | A recovery or reset quarter can legitimately hold a single priority |
| A measure counts effort (sent, posted, held, attempted) | Run the linter: ask what the effort was meant to move, and rewrite the measure as that outcome, keeping the activity as a listed tactic if useful | The member, told plainly why it is an activity measure, can keep it; label it "activity measure, member's call" in the plan |
| A priority has no single named owner or no deadline | It is not a priority yet. Hold it out of the plan until both exist. Shared ownership counts as no ownership | Household lens: the shared family priority names one coordinating adult, and that satisfies the rule |
| No diagnosis or guiding direction exists in `strategy-os/memory/strategy.md` | Flag it, recommend running strategy-kernel first, and proceed only if the member says so, marking the plan "set without a standing strategy" | A professional whose direction is set above her head plans against the stated organisational direction instead |
| A priority is a launch | Name WHAT launches and declare the launch window in the plan; hand the HOW entirely to the campaign layer | None. This skill never designs the campaign |
| A priority reaches into another department's craft (positioning, funnel, pipeline detail) | Write the goal line at outcome level and leave the craft to that department's skills; read only aggregated patterns from Sales | None |
| A measure has a target but no warning threshold | Add a proposed threshold (the number at which the quarter is off track), because admin-ops scorecards will consume it weekly | Member may set or amend any threshold |
| A number is quoted (baseline, market size, benchmark) with no source | Mark it "unverified" in the plan or trace it to a named, opened source; never present it as fact | None. This never bends |
| The member asks for the family or real-life quarter | Apply the household lens: one shared priority plus one per person, same owner/measure/deadline shape, gentler cadence | None |

## 5. Workflow

1. Read the inputs (Section 9): the standing direction, last quarter's plan and how it ended, the market landscape, and the member's context file. Note the primary objective, anything carried over, and anything the review flagged for reallocation.
2. Gather candidates with the member: what must be true in 90 days? List everything first, without judging.
3. Force-rank to at most 7. For each cut, name where it goes: next quarter, the stop-doing list, or a department's business-as-usual. The implicit move: watch for the priority nobody will own, because that is usually the one that should be cut.
4. Shape each survivor into a priority line: one owner, one outcome measure, a target, a warning threshold, a deadline inside the quarter.
5. Run the outcome-vs-activity linter on every measure. Test: "if all this effort happened and nothing changed, is the quarter still a success?" If yes, it is an activity measure; rewrite it as the outcome it was meant to move (for example "send 20 partnership emails" becomes "2 signed partnership agreements", with the emails kept as a tactic).
6. Name the single primary objective for the quarter, then write the per-department goal lines (department, goal, metric, target, deadline) and declare any launch windows.
7. Draft the full plan into `strategy-os/memory/quarterly-plan.md` (Section 10), and draft the synced summary for `memory/business-context.md` as an exact diff block the member applies themselves. Never edit the context file directly.
8. Present both for approval, log the session, and note anything held out and why.

## 6. Autonomy tiers

- **Always safe (Green: act, then log):** read the named memory files, gather and rank candidates, run the linter, draft the quarterly plan, draft the context-file diff block, list what was cut and where it went.
- **Draft and wait for approval (Amber):** writing the approved plan to `strategy-os/memory/quarterly-plan.md`; the diff block for `memory/business-context.md` (the member applies it, always); anything that restates a department's targets to that department.
- **Never (no matter the tier):** exceed 7 priorities; write a priority without an owner, a measure, and a deadline; invent a baseline, benchmark, or market statistic; edit `memory/business-context.md` directly; decide a goal on the member's behalf; design how a launch runs; assign or route work to people or departments (the CEO layer owns routing); delete a prior quarter's plan (archive, never erase); move money or commit to spend.

## 7. Escalation

If the standing strategy is missing, stale, or contradicts what the member is asking for, stop and raise it in the session before drafting: a quarter planned against a dead strategy wastes 90 days. If two proposed priorities conflict (one needs the same capacity the other consumes), present the conflict as a decision for the member rather than quietly picking. If a department's goal line implies a target its own history says is implausible, flag it with the evidence and let the member set it anyway if they choose, logged as their call. Routine matters (cuts made, linter rewrites, thresholds proposed) go in the activity log; anything held out, overridden, or flagged goes in the decision log with the reason.

## 8. Responsible use

This skill's real failure modes: activity theatre (a plan full of effort measures that lets everyone finish busy and fail quietly; the linter exists to stop it); goal inflation (stretching the cap "just this once", which is how seven becomes twelve and the plan stops meaning anything); fabricated confidence (a target or baseline stated as fact with no source; every load-bearing number is either traced to a named, opened source or labelled unverified); and quiet authority creep (the skill deciding instead of proposing). Strategy documents are drafted for the member; the decisions are the member's, every time. When a plan or goal line is shared beyond the member, be transparent that AI assisted in drafting it, in line with the member's disclosure preference.

## 9. Inputs and memory

- **Reads:** `memory/business-context.md` (the founder, professional, or household variant, whichever the member built): who the member is, departments, capacity, overridable thresholds; `strategy-os/memory/strategy.md`: the standing diagnosis and direction the quarter must serve; `strategy-os/memory/quarterly-plan.md`: the outgoing quarter, carried-over priorities, and the standing stop-doing list; `strategy-os/memory/market-landscape.md`: the current external read, where one exists; `strategy-os/memory/strategy-settings.md` (this department's own settings: her priority cap, which this skill enforces; the financial year start; and the numbers that matter).
- **Writes:** `strategy-os/memory/quarterly-plan.md` (the canonical home of goals, on approval); an exact diff block for `memory/business-context.md` (member-applied, never direct); `logs/activity-log.md` (the session and what was drafted); `logs/decision-log.md` (cuts, overrides, unverified numbers kept, anything flagged).

Never read "any relevant context". Read the named files above.

## 11. What good looks like

**Good example (annotated).**

> **Priority 2: Corporate training becomes a reliable second income line.** Owner: Nadia. [1] Measure: 3 signed corporate training agreements (was "send 20 outreach emails", rewritten by the linter; outreach kept as a tactic). [2] Target: 3 signed. Warning threshold: fewer than 1 signed by week 6. [3] Deadline: 26 September. Launch window declared: corporate offer launches weeks 3 to 5; campaign design handed to the campaign layer. [4]

1. One named owner. Not "the team", not two names: accountability has an address, per the Wickman pattern.
2. The linter at work, per Doerr and Wodtke: the effort ("send 20 emails") is rewritten as the outcome it was meant to move, and you can now fail at this even after sending every email, which is what makes it a real measure.
3. A warning threshold with a date, so the weekly scorecard (admin-ops) can catch drift at week 6 instead of discovering failure at week 13.
4. The boundary held: this skill declared WHAT launches and when; it did not write a single line of the campaign.

The same shape across audiences: a **professional** writes "customer onboarding time under 10 days (from 24)" instead of "hold 8 process workshops"; in **real life**, the shared household priority reads "the mortgage refinanced by 30 September, Priya coordinating", not "look into rates".

**Bad example (named failure mode: activity theatre plus goal inflation).**

> "Q3 priorities (12): post daily on socials, send 20 partner emails, run 6 webinars, be more visible, update the website, do more networking, grow the list, improve operations..."

Failure mode: twelve "priorities" with no owners, no deadlines, and every measure counting effort or, worse, unmeasurable ("be more visible"). Everyone could complete all of it and the business would be exactly where it started, which is Wodtke's cargo-cult warning made real. The skill must refuse this list: force-rank to 7 or fewer, rewrite each survivor as an owned, dated outcome, and put the rest on the stop-doing list where they stop leaking attention.
