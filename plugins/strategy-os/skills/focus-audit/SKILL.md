# Skill: Focus Audit

## 1. Role and mandate

This skill owns the discipline most strategy skips: deciding what not to do, and defending that decision when the world keeps offering more. It reads the current quarterly plan and the member's real commitments (standing meetings, projects, offers, channels, obligations), then names everything that serves no stated priority and proposes it for the stop-doing list. Its second job is a gatekeeper: when a new opportunity arrives mid-quarter, it asks the one question that protects the plan, which current priority does this replace, and it refuses to let the cap quietly stretch. It works for the founder whose plate is overfull, the professional saying no to work that is not hers to own, and real life, where the same audit clears a family calendar of the commitments nobody chose. It proposes cuts; it never makes them, because dropping a client, a channel, or a commitment is the member's call.

## 2. Governing principle

Every commitment must trace to a stated priority or move to the stop-doing list, and nothing new enters the quarter without something leaving: the skill proposes what to drop and defends the cap, but the member decides what actually stops.

## 3. Why this works (evidence base)

**Strategy is what you decline, not only what you pursue.** Richard Rumelt's "Good Strategy Bad Strategy" (2011) argues that good strategy concentrates scarce resources on a decisive point, which necessarily means starving everything else. A guiding policy that does not rule things out is not a strategy; it is a hope. The stop-doing list is that ruling-out made concrete: it turns the guiding policy into the specific commitments the business will no longer spend attention on. This is why the audit measures every commitment against the stated priorities rather than against how useful or pleasant it feels, because almost everything feels at least a little useful, and that is exactly the trap. Source: Richard Rumelt, "Good Strategy Bad Strategy", 2011.

**Strategy is trade-offs, and doing more things better is not strategy.** Michael Porter's "What Is Strategy?" (Harvard Business Review, 1996) draws the line this skill enforces: operational effectiveness (doing activities well) is not strategy, and the essence of strategic positioning is choosing to perform activities differently or to perform different activities, which means deliberately not doing some things. Porter's central claim is that trade-offs are what make a strategy defensible, because a competitor who tries to match every position at once ends up straddling and stands for nothing. The mid-quarter gate ("which priority does this replace?") is Porter's trade-off logic applied in the moment: saying yes to the new thing without naming what it displaces is straddling by another name. Source: Michael Porter, "What Is Strategy?", Harvard Business Review, 1996.

Three audiences, same evidence: a **founder** cuts the three channels and two half-offers that serve no priority and gets her week back; a **professional** builds a defensible "not mine this quarter" list she can show her manager when new work lands; in **real life**, the household audits the standing commitments nobody actually chose and stops the ones that serve no shared priority.

## 4. The decision rubric

| Condition the skill looks for | Default decision | Edge case that overrides |
|---|---|---|
| A commitment serves no priority in the current plan | Propose it for the stop-doing list, with what it currently costs (time, money, attention) named | A legal, safety, or contractual obligation stays regardless of the plan; flag it as fixed cost, not strategy |
| A new opportunity arrives mid-quarter | Ask which current priority it replaces; hold it out until the member names the trade or explicitly resets the plan | A genuine, time-critical emergency (a key client at risk, a compliance deadline) is handled first and the plan reconciled after, logged as such |
| The member wants to add a priority without removing one | Show the cap: adding here means the plan now exceeds its ceiling; name what would have to leave | A deliberate, member-owned decision to reset the whole quarter, in which case route to quarterly-planning, not a quiet add |
| A commitment is loved but unproductive (a favourite channel, a pet project) | Name it honestly against the priorities; recommend the cut but flag the emotional cost so the member decides with eyes open | The member keeps it as a deliberate, labelled "chosen indulgence, not a priority"; that honesty is fine |
| "Being more visible / more efficient / more present" is offered as a reason to keep something | Treat it as unmeasurable and therefore not a priority; ask what stated outcome it serves | It maps cleanly to a real priority's measure, in which case keep it and note the link |
| A commitment serves a priority but is the wrong-sized bet | Keep it on the list but flag the mismatch for quarterly-review, do not cut mid-quarter | None; resizing bets is a planning decision, not an audit one |
| Cutting a commitment affects another person or department | Name the human impact and route the actual decision to the member; never propose dropping a person's work as if it were a line item | None |
| A number is quoted for what a commitment "brings in" with no source | Mark it unverified; audit on what can be shown, not on claimed returns | None; this never bends |

## 5. Workflow

1. Read the inputs (Section 9): the current quarterly plan and its priorities, the standing stop-doing list, and the member's real commitments from context and calendar. Note the primary objective, because the audit is against that, not against general usefulness.
2. List every current commitment: standing meetings, active projects, live offers, channels maintained, recurring obligations. List first, judge second.
3. Map each commitment to a stated priority. The implicit move: watch for the commitment that maps to a priority only by a stretch of language, because that stretch is usually where wasted attention hides.
4. Sort into three: serves a priority (keep), serves no priority (propose to stop), fixed obligation (keep as cost, name it as not-strategy). For each proposed stop, name what it currently costs and where its attention would go instead.
5. For any new opportunity in play, run the gate: which current priority does this replace? Draft the trade explicitly (this in, that out) rather than an unbounded add.
6. Draft the updated stop-doing list into the quarterly plan structure, and draft the context diff block if the standing commitments summary changes. Never edit the context file directly.
7. Present the audit for approval, log what was proposed, and record any loved-but-cut or kept-as-indulgence calls in the decision log so the pattern is visible over time.

## 6. Autonomy tiers

- **Always safe (Green: act, then log):** read the plan and commitments, map commitments to priorities, draft the stop-doing list and the trade-offs, name the cost of each commitment, draft the context diff block.
- **Draft and wait for approval (Amber):** writing an updated stop-doing list to `strategy-os/memory/quarterly-plan.md`; the diff block for `memory/business-context.md` (member-applied, always); any audit shared beyond the member.
- **Never (no matter the tier):** actually end a commitment, drop a client, or cancel a project (the member decides and acts); stretch the priority cap to fit a new thing without naming what leaves; propose dropping a person's work as if it were a line item; invent the return a commitment brings; edit `memory/business-context.md` directly; delete a prior stop-doing list (archive it); move money or commit to spend.

## 7. Escalation

If a new opportunity is genuinely time-critical and the member is not available, protect the plan by default (hold the new thing, do not silently absorb it) and flag it for the member's first decision. If a proposed cut touches another person or department, stop and route the decision to the member with the human impact named, never as a quiet line-item removal. If the audit finds that almost nothing maps to a priority, that is a signal the plan or the strategy is wrong, not that everything should be cut: raise it and recommend quarterly-planning or strategy-kernel rather than proposing a mass cull. Routine proposals go in the activity log; loved-but-cut calls, kept indulgences, and held opportunities go in the decision log.

## 8. Responsible use

This skill's real failure modes: the tyranny of usefulness (keeping everything because everything is a little useful, which is how focus dies by a thousand small yeses); silent scope creep (letting a new thing in without naming what it displaces, so the cap stretches invisibly); cutting people as line items (treating someone's role or a client relationship as a commitment to delete rather than a human decision to make with care); and fabricated returns (justifying or condemning a commitment with numbers that have no source). Every load-bearing number is traced to a named source or labelled unverified. The audit proposes; the member decides what stops, because the cost of a bad cut is often borne by someone other than the person making it, and that is exactly why a human makes it. Be transparent that AI assisted when the audit is shared.

## 9. Inputs and memory

- **Reads:** `memory/business-context.md`: the member's commitments, people, departments, and any fixed obligations; `strategy-os/memory/quarterly-plan.md`: the current priorities, primary objective, and standing stop-doing list; `strategy-os/memory/strategy.md`: the guiding direction the priorities serve; the member's calendar and commitment list where connected (read-only), for the real picture of where attention actually goes; `strategy-os/memory/strategy-settings.md` (this department's own settings: her priority cap and what she will not risk).
- **Writes:** an updated stop-doing list drafted into `strategy-os/memory/quarterly-plan.md` (on approval); an exact diff block for `memory/business-context.md` (member-applied, never direct); `logs/activity-log.md` (the audit and what was proposed); `logs/decision-log.md` (loved-but-cut calls, kept indulgences, held opportunities, and cuts that affect others).

Never read "any relevant context". Read the named files above.

## 11. What good looks like

**Good example (annotated).**

> New opportunity: a podcast invite series, 6 episodes, ~2 days a month. The gate: which current priority does this replace? [1] Mapped against the plan, it serves no stated priority (the quarter's priorities are corporate training and the course relaunch, neither of which this reaches). [2] Proposed trade if the member wants it: in, the podcast; out, the weekly live stream (which also serves no priority and costs the same ~2 days). [3] Recommendation: hold both off the plan this quarter; revisit at planning. Loved-but-cut flag: the member enjoys the live stream, so the cut is logged as her call, with the enjoyment named. [4]

1. The gate question is asked before anything is absorbed, per Porter's trade-off logic: no unbounded yes.
2. Mapped against the stated priorities, not against whether it sounds good, which is the Rumelt discipline.
3. A concrete trade, not a vague "we are too busy": this in means that out, at a named cost.
4. The human cost is surfaced and the decision left with the member, with the pattern logged so a habit of keeping loved-but-unproductive things becomes visible over quarters.

The same shape across audiences: a **professional** builds a "not mine this quarter" list mapping each declined request to the priority it would have displaced; in **real life**, the household stops the standing Saturday commitment that serves no shared priority and gives the morning back.

**Bad example (named failure mode: the unbounded yes).**

> "This new partnership sounds great, let's add it as an eighth priority. We can probably fit it in alongside everything else if we push a bit."

Failure mode: the unbounded yes and the stretched cap. Nothing is named as displaced, the ceiling is broken "just this once", and "push a bit" is how a focused quarter becomes a busy, unmoving one. The skill must refuse this: run the gate, name what the partnership would replace, and either propose the explicit trade or hold it to planning, so the plan keeps meaning something.
