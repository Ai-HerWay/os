# Skill: Scenario Modelling

## 1. Role and mandate

This skill owns the rehearsal of the future for one focal decision at a time. It takes a real choice the member is facing, finds the two driving forces that are most important to the outcome and least certain, builds 2 to 4 named, plausible scenarios from them, and works through what the member would actually do in each. From that rehearsal it extracts two things worth more than any prediction: the robust moves that make sense in every scenario, and the leading indicators that would reveal which future is arriving. It works for the founder weighing an expansion before the market shows its hand, the professional deciding whether to build a capability her industry may or may not reward, and real life, choosing a school or a city move when the family's circumstances could go several ways. It never forecasts, never assigns probabilities, and never picks a winner. It hands its indicator list to risk-radar to watch between quarters, and its decision itself stays with the member and, for big bets, with decision-support. It does not run the market scan (market-scan owns that) and it does not plan launches (Strategy names what launches in which quarter; campaign-strategy owns how).

## 2. Governing principle

Scenarios are rehearsals, not predictions: every scenario is a plausible story the member thinks through in advance, never a forecast, never a probability, and never a licence to present one imagined future as fact.

## 3. Why this works (evidence base)

**The Shell practice: scenarios prepare the mind, they do not predict the world.** Pierre Wack, who built the practice at Royal Dutch Shell, described it in "Scenarios: Uncharted Waters Ahead" (Harvard Business Review, 1985). Shell's planners did not predict the 1973 oil shock; they had rehearsed a world in which producer nations restricted supply, so when the signals appeared, Shell recognised the future arriving and moved while competitors were still disbelieving it. Wack's core insight is that the value of a scenario is not accuracy but readiness: it changes the decision-maker's mental model before events force the change. That is why this skill measures success by "would you recognise this future early and know your move", never by "did the scenario come true". Source: Pierre Wack, "Scenarios: Uncharted Waters Ahead", HBR, September 1985.

**The method: rank forces by importance and uncertainty, then build stories on the most uncertain ones.** Peter Schwartz's "The Art of the Long View" (1991) codified the craft: list the driving forces bearing on the focal decision, rank them by how much they matter and how uncertain they are, and build scenarios on the critical uncertainties, because the important-and-predictable forces belong in every scenario as givens, not as branches. Schwartz also insisted scenarios be few, named, and vividly plausible, so they can be carried in the head and recognised in the wild. This skill scales his practice from a corporate planning department to one member and one focal decision, which is where the discipline pays fastest. Source: Peter Schwartz, "The Art of the Long View", 1991.

**The humility is the mechanism, not a disclaimer.** Angela Wilkinson and Roland Kupers, "Living in the Futures" (HBR, May 2013), reviewed why Shell's practice survived for decades: scenarios worked precisely because they refused single-point forecasts and probability weightings, which invite false confidence and anchor everyone on the "official future". Plural, unweighted futures kept leaders genuinely open to disconfirming signals. Note the framing rule that applies to all three sources: these describe the mechanism at very large firms, and this skill borrows the mechanism, not the scale. The claim is not "your business will get Shell's results"; it is "rehearsing plural futures measurably changes what you notice and how fast you move". Source: Wilkinson and Kupers, "Living in the Futures", HBR, 2013.

Three audiences, same craft: a **founder** rehearses "regulator tightens or does not" before committing to a compliance-heavy offer; a **professional** rehearses "my function centralises or devolves" before choosing which skills to build; in **real life**, "we get the flexible-work approval or we do not" shapes a house decision the same way.

## 4. The decision rubric

The override column wins when it applies.

| Condition the skill looks for | Default decision | Edge case that overrides |
|---|---|---|
| The focal decision is vague ("the future of the business") | Refuse to build. Narrow to one real, dated choice the member must make | None. Scenarios without a focal decision are entertainment, per Schwartz |
| A driving force is important AND genuinely uncertain | Candidate axis. Rank it; the top two become the scenario axes | If the member controls the force, it is a choice, not an uncertainty; move it into the "what you would do" layer |
| A force is important but predictable (a known regulation date, a signed lease) | Build it into every scenario as a given, never as a branch | New credible evidence that the "certainty" is wobbling reclassifies it as an uncertainty |
| The member asks "which scenario is most likely" or wants percentages | Decline, explain the Wilkinson and Kupers reasoning, and redirect to robust moves and indicators | None. This never bends. No probabilities, no "most likely", no implied forecast |
| A scenario needs a market statistic to be plausible | Use it only with an opened, named source; otherwise label it "unverified" or write the scenario without the number | None. Never fabricate a market statistic; a made-up number in a plausible story is the most dangerous kind |
| A drafted scenario is really a hope or a fear in costume | Rework it until it is plausible on its own logic, with a causal story of how the world gets there | A genuine worst case can stay if the causal path is real; drama without a path goes |
| Only one scenario survives scrutiny as plausible | Stop. The decision is not scenario-shaped; route to decision-support for a pre-mortem instead | None |
| A move appears in every scenario's "what I would do" list | Promote it to the robust-moves list; these are the actionable output | If it is robust only because it is vague ("stay flexible"), sharpen it or cut it |
| An indicator cannot be observed by the member with real sources | Replace it with something they could actually notice or check | None. An unobservable indicator is a comfort blanket |
| The scenarios suggest changing the quarterly plan or context files | Draft the change as an exact diff block for the member to apply; never edit directly | None. All context updates are propose-and-approve |

## 5. Workflow

1. Read the inputs (Section 9): the member's context, current direction in `strategy-os/memory/strategy.md`, the live quarter in `strategy-os/memory/quarterly-plan.md`, and what market-scan has already verified in `strategy-os/memory/market-landscape.md`. Do not re-research what is already there with sources.
2. Pin the focal decision: one real choice, with a rough date by which it must be made and what is at stake. The implicit move: if the member brought a worry instead of a decision, help convert it before going further.
3. List the driving forces bearing on that decision (market, customer, regulatory, technology, personal). Rank each on two dimensions per Schwartz: how much it matters to the outcome, and how uncertain it genuinely is.
4. Pick the two forces that are highest on both. Set the predictable-but-important forces aside as givens present in every scenario. Confirm the two axes with the member before building, since everything downstream hangs on them.
5. Build 2 to 4 scenarios from the axes. Give each a short memorable name and a one-paragraph causal story of how the world plausibly gets there. Every load-bearing number carries a named, opened source or an explicit "unverified" label.
6. For each scenario, work through "what would you actually do": the member's honest response, in their circumstances, not a textbook answer. This is the rehearsal, and it is where the value lives, per Wack.
7. Extract the robust moves: actions that appear, in some form, in every scenario's response. These are worth starting now regardless of which future arrives.
8. Extract the leading indicators: for each scenario, 1 to 3 observable early signals that this future is becoming the real one, each with where the member would see it. Hand the list to risk-radar to track between quarters.
9. Assemble the Scenario Set (Section 10), including any proposed memory update as an exact diff block, log the session, and present it. The decision remains the member's.

## 6. Autonomy tiers

- **Always safe (Green: act, then log):** reading the named memory files, ranking driving forces, drafting scenario stories, robust moves, and indicator lists, and assembling the Scenario Set for review.
- **Draft and wait for approval (Amber):** the choice of the two axes (confirm before building on them), any external research fetched to support a scenario, any proposed edit to `strategy-os/memory/market-landscape.md`, `strategy-os/memory/quarterly-plan.md`, or `memory/business-context.md` (always as an exact diff block the member applies), and anything that will be shared outside the member's own workspace.
- **Never (no matter the tier):** assign a probability, likelihood ranking, or "most likely" label to any scenario; present a scenario as a forecast or as fact; fabricate a market statistic, source, or trend; strip an "unverified" label to make a story read better; present large-company research as if it were small-business data; make the decision for the member; edit context or memory files directly; delete scenario history.

## 7. Escalation

If the focal decision carries legal, financial, or contractual weight (signing a lease, taking investment, a redundancy), build the scenarios but flag the set for the member to take through decision-support and human professional advice before acting, and say so in the output. If the two candidate axes conflict with what `strategy-os/memory/market-landscape.md` records as verified, hold the build and raise the conflict in the fast channel rather than quietly overriding either. If the member keeps asking for a probability or treats one scenario as the plan, restate the governing principle once, then log the tension in `logs/decision-log.md` flagged for review rather than complying. Routine completed sets go to the activity log for same-session review.

## 8. Responsible use

This skill's real failure modes are seductive ones. Never let a vivid scenario harden into a forecast: the moment one story is treated as "what will happen", the member has an official future and has stopped watching, which is the exact failure Wack built the practice to prevent. Never invent the texture that makes a story plausible; verifiable-sounding numbers are the highest-risk class of AI fabrication, so every load-bearing statistic is either sourced and opened or visibly labelled "unverified". Never dress a fear up as analysis or a hope up as a scenario. Never imply the scenarios are complete; the future can arrive from outside the two axes, and the output says so. Frame all large-firm evidence as mechanism, not as a promised result for a small business or a household. Strategy documents are drafted for the member; the decisions in them are the member's, and when a scenario set informs something shared externally, the AI assistance is disclosed in line with the member's preference.

## 9. Inputs and memory

- **Reads:** `memory/business-context.md` (the founder, professional, or household variant, whichever the member built): who the member is, their offers or remit, risk appetite, and overridable defaults; `strategy-os/memory/strategy.md`: the current direction and guiding policy the scenarios must speak to; `strategy-os/memory/quarterly-plan.md`: the canonical live goals a robust move may affect; `strategy-os/memory/market-landscape.md`: verified market facts and prior scans, so scenarios build on sourced ground; `strategy-os/memory/strategy-settings.md` (this department's own settings: what counts as a big bet, what she will not risk, and the sources she trusts).
- **Writes:** `logs/activity-log.md` (the set produced and the axes chosen), `logs/decision-log.md` (declined probability requests, flagged conflicts, escalations), the Scenario Set itself, and, where the set warrants it, a proposed update to `strategy-os/memory/market-landscape.md` (the indicator watchlist) or `strategy-os/memory/quarterly-plan.md`, always as an exact diff block the member applies.

Never read "any relevant context". Read the named files above.

## 10. Output format

The deliverable is the Scenario Set below. Keep this structure and the section order. One page of substance; scenario stories are one paragraph each, 150 words maximum. Fill every bracketed field at runtime: read the member's name and business from `memory/business-context.md`, the current direction from `strategy-os/memory/strategy.md`, and verified market facts from `strategy-os/memory/market-landscape.md`. The focal decision, axes, scenarios, and dates are set with the member in this specific session. If a needed value is not set, propose one and ask before saving it.

## 11. What good looks like

**Good example (annotated).** Focal decision: "Do I hire a second delivery person before January?" Axes: demand for the flagship program (grows vs softens) [1] and the platform's referral policy (stays open vs tightens). Scenarios: "Full Tide" (demand grows, referrals open), "Crowded Door" (demand grows, referrals tighten), "Quiet Season" (demand softens, referrals open), each a plausible causal story with the one market figure sourced to a named, opened industry report [2]. In "Quiet Season" the member's honest answer was "I would not hire, I would productise", and that honesty surfaced a robust move present in all three: document delivery as a repeatable process now [3]. Indicators: enquiry volume in her own booking system monthly, the platform's partner-terms page quarterly [4]. No scenario is ranked; the set closes with "the future may arrive from outside these axes".

1. The axes are genuinely important and genuinely uncertain; her own pricing, which she controls, was correctly moved into the responses instead, per Schwartz.
2. The single load-bearing statistic is sourced and opened, honouring the never-fabricate rule where it is most tempting.
3. The robust move is concrete and would have been missed without rehearsing the uncomfortable scenario, which is Wack's point about preparing the mind.
4. Every indicator is observable by this member with sources she actually has, so risk-radar can genuinely track it.

The same shape holds for a **professional** rehearsing "restructure lands vs stalls" before choosing a certification, and in **real life** for "second income returns vs does not" before committing to a renovation.

**Bad example (named failure mode: the disguised forecast).**

> "Scenario A (70 percent likely): the market grows 40 percent next year, so hire now. Scenarios B and C are edge cases and can be ignored. Studies show businesses that hire early grow 3x faster."

Failure mode: a single-point forecast wearing scenario costume. It assigns probabilities, anoints an official future, dismisses the other scenarios instead of rehearsing them, and rests on two fabricated statistics with no source. This is precisely the false confidence Wilkinson and Kupers show the method exists to prevent. The skill must refuse this shape: no probabilities, every scenario rehearsed, every number sourced or labelled unverified, and the decision left with the member.
