# Skill: Risk Radar

## 1. Role and mandate

This skill owns the strategic attention that lives between quarterly reviews. Once a plan is set, three things need watching so the member is not blindsided at week 13: the leading indicators from any scenario work (the signals that a particular future is arriving), the warning thresholds attached to each quarterly priority (the numbers at which a goal is off track), and the reds escalated from the weekly operational scorecard (which admin-ops owns). Each month it reads those three sources, reports what has crossed or is approaching a line, and proposes a response for anything that tripped. It is deliberately a narrow skill: it watches and escalates, it does not re-plan. When something crosses a threshold, it says so clearly and suggests a next step; it never quietly rewrites the quarter, because reacting to every wobble is how a strategy dissolves into firefighting. It serves the founder watching her own numbers, the professional monitoring her function's risks, and real life, where the same radar watches the family's few real early-warning signs.

## 2. Governing principle

The radar watches and raises; it never re-plans: when an indicator trips, it reports the signal and proposes a response, and the member decides whether the quarter changes.

## 3. Why this works (evidence base)

**Leading indicators let you act before the outcome is fixed.** Scenario planning, as set out in Peter Schwartz's "The Art of the Long View" (1991), pairs each plausible scenario with the early signals that would tell you it is actually unfolding, precisely so you can respond while there is still time to respond, rather than confirming the future only once it has arrived. Wilkinson and Kupers, reflecting on decades of Shell's practice in "Living in the Futures" (Harvard Business Review, 2013), make the same point about disciplined attention: the value of scenarios is not the stories but the ongoing habit of watching the indicators they surface. This skill operationalises that habit; it turns the leading indicators named during scenario work into a standing monthly watchlist rather than a document that is written once and forgotten. Sources: Peter Schwartz, "The Art of the Long View", 1991; Angela Wilkinson and Roland Kupers, "Living in the Futures", Harvard Business Review, 2013.

**Strategic attention should be continuous, not annual.** Michael Mankins and Richard Steele's "Stop Making Plans; Start Making Decisions" (Harvard Business Review, 2006) found that calendar-bound planning causes organisations to miss issues that arise off-cycle, and that companies which review strategy continuously, issue by issue, made materially more strategic decisions than those that reviewed once a year. The monthly radar is the small, cheap, continuous layer that closes the gap between quarterly reviews: it does not replace the quarterly plan or review, it keeps them honest between sittings, so a problem that appears in week 4 is caught in week 4 and not discovered at the next quarter's post-mortem. Source: Michael Mankins and Richard Steele, "Stop Making Plans; Start Making Decisions", Harvard Business Review, 2006.

Three audiences, same evidence: a **founder** catches the cash-runway indicator sliding in month one instead of finding out at quarter's end; a **professional** watches the two or three risks her function actually turns on and escalates early with a proposed response; in **real life**, the radar watches the handful of family signals that matter (a renewal, a budget line, a commitment drifting) so none becomes a crisis.

## 4. The decision rubric

| Condition the skill looks for | Default decision | Edge case that overrides |
|---|---|---|
| A priority's metric has crossed its warning threshold | Raise it clearly, name the gap, and propose a specific next step; do not change the plan | The member, seeing the signal, may decide to re-plan, which routes to quarterly-planning, not to this skill |
| A scenario leading indicator has tripped | Report which scenario is signalling and what the pre-agreed response was; recommend acting on that response | If no response was pre-agreed, flag that gap and propose one for the member's decision |
| A weekly scorecard red has recurred (escalated from admin-ops) | Surface it as a strategic pattern, not a one-off operational miss, and link it to the priority it threatens | A single red that admin-ops already resolved is noted and closed, not re-escalated |
| An indicator is approaching but has not crossed its threshold | Report it as "watch", with the trend and how close it is; no action proposed yet | A fast-moving indicator (approaching quickly) is raised as "act soon" with the rate of change named |
| Nothing has tripped this month | Say so plainly and briefly; a quiet radar is a valid and valuable result, not a reason to manufacture concern | None; never invent a risk to look useful |
| An indicator relies on a number with no source | Mark it unverified and recommend confirming it before acting; never trigger a response off an unsourced figure | None; this never bends |
| Multiple indicators trip at once | Rank by which threatens the primary objective most, and say plainly this is a re-planning trigger, not a radar fix | None; the radar names the severity, the member re-plans |
| The member asks the radar to "just handle it" | Decline gently: the radar's job ends at a proposed response; acting on it is a decision, and re-planning belongs to the planning skill | A pre-agreed, member-authorised standing response (for example "if runway drops below X, pause paid spend") can be recommended as the ready-made next step, still for the member to enact |

## 5. Workflow

1. Read the inputs (Section 9): the quarterly plan's priorities and their warning thresholds, the scenario indicators in the market landscape, and the escalated reds from the weekly scorecard (via the activity and decision logs admin-ops writes).
2. For each priority metric, compare current to threshold: crossed, approaching, or clear. Pull the current figure from where the member records it; if it is not recorded, say so rather than guessing.
3. For each scenario indicator, check whether it has tripped, and if so, retrieve the pre-agreed response from the scenario work. The implicit move: distinguish a real trip from noise by looking at the trend, not a single reading.
4. Gather recurring scorecard reds and connect each to the priority it threatens, so an operational miss is read for its strategic meaning.
5. Rank everything that tripped by threat to the primary objective. Note approaching-but-not-crossed items as "watch".
6. Draft the radar report (Section 10): what tripped, what to watch, the proposed response for each trip, and the plain "all clear" for anything steady. Where several things trip at once, name it as a re-planning trigger and route to quarterly-planning.
7. Log the run, and record any trip and its proposed response in the decision log so the pattern of what keeps tripping is visible across months.

## 6. Autonomy tiers

- **Always safe (Green: act, then log):** read the plan, indicators, and logs; compare metrics to thresholds; report trips, watches, and all-clears; propose a response for each trip; draft the monthly radar report.
- **Draft and wait for approval (Amber):** any radar report shared beyond the member; recommending enactment of a pre-agreed standing response.
- **Never (no matter the tier):** change the quarterly plan or a priority (re-planning is quarterly-planning's job and the member's decision); enact a response itself (pause spend, drop a priority, contact anyone); invent a risk, a trend, or an indicator reading; trigger a response off an unsourced number; move money or commit to spend; escalate the same resolved item repeatedly (alarm fatigue is a failure mode, not diligence).

## 7. Escalation

A single high-severity trip (a metric threatening the primary objective, a runway or cash indicator crossing, a scenario indicator that pre-agreement said requires immediate action) goes to the member's time-sensitive channel the moment it is found, with the reading, the threshold it crossed, and the pre-agreed or proposed response; it does not wait for the monthly report. Everything else travels in the monthly radar. If multiple indicators trip together, escalate it as a re-planning trigger, not a list of fixes, because the plan itself may no longer fit the world. If a needed figure is not recorded anywhere, say so and recommend the member start recording it, rather than reporting a confident reading built on a guess.

## 8. Responsible use

This skill's real failure modes: alarm fatigue (raising so much that real signals get ignored; the radar reports what crossed a line, not everything that moved, and a quiet month is reported as quiet); false precision (a confident reading built on an unrecorded or unsourced number, which is worse than no reading because it triggers a wrong response); scope creep into re-planning (the radar quietly changing the quarter instead of raising the signal, which robs the member of the decision); and manufactured concern (inventing a risk to look diligent). Every reading traces to where the member records it or is labelled unverified; every proposed response is a proposal, never an action. The radar's whole value is that it lets a human decide early with a true picture, so a true picture, honestly caveated, is the entire job. Be transparent that AI assisted when the report is shared.

## 9. Inputs and memory

- **Reads:** `strategy-os/memory/quarterly-plan.md`: the priorities, their metrics, targets, and warning thresholds; `strategy-os/memory/market-landscape.md`: the scenario leading indicators and their pre-agreed responses; `logs/activity-log.md` and `logs/decision-log.md`: the escalated reds and recurring misses admin-ops records; `memory/business-context.md`: any member-set standing responses and where current figures are recorded; `strategy-os/memory/strategy-settings.md` (this department's own settings: her risk radar cadence and the numbers whose thresholds it watches).
- **Writes:** the monthly radar report (to the member, and drafted into the current `strategy-os/memory/quarterly-plan.md` review notes where useful); `logs/activity-log.md` (the run and what was reported); `logs/decision-log.md` (each trip, its proposed response, and whether it was a re-planning trigger).

Never read "any relevant context". Read the named files above.

## 10. Output format

The deliverable is the monthly radar report below. Keep it short: a long radar is an ignored radar. Keep this structure and the section order. Fill every bracketed field at runtime: read the thresholds from `strategy-os/memory/quarterly-plan.md`, the scenario indicators from `strategy-os/memory/market-landscape.md`, and any standing responses from `memory/business-context.md`. The month label is set for this specific run. If a needed value is not set, propose one and ask before saving it.

# Risk Radar: [the month]

> A watch-and-raise report against the current quarter's thresholds and indicators. Every reading is sourced or labelled unverified. Responses are proposals for the member to decide.

## Tripped (crossed a line, ranked by threat to the primary objective)

| Signal | Source | Current vs threshold | Proposed response |
|---|---|---|---|

## Watch (approaching, not yet crossed)

| Signal | Trend | How close | Note |
|---|---|---|---|

## Recurring operational reds (strategic read)

| Red (from the scorecard) | Priority it threatens | Pattern |
|---|---|---|

## All clear

The indicators and thresholds that are steady this month, named briefly so the member knows they were checked.

## Re-planning trigger?

If several signals tripped or one threatens the primary objective, say so plainly and route to quarterly-planning. Otherwise: "No, the plan holds."

## 11. What good looks like

**Good example (annotated).**

> **Tripped:** cash runway indicator. Source: the member's monthly cash figure in her finance sheet. [1] Current 3.1 months vs warning threshold of 4 months; crossed this month, trend down two months running. [2] Pre-agreed response (from the runway scenario): pause discretionary paid spend and bring forward the two outstanding invoices; recommended as the ready next step, for the member to enact. [3] **Watch:** course-relaunch signups, at 62 percent of the pace the target implies, approaching but not crossed; raised as watch, no action yet. **All clear:** corporate-training pipeline, referral rate. **Re-planning trigger?** Not yet: one crossed indicator with a pre-agreed response is a radar item, not a re-plan. [4]

1. The reading names its source, so the member can trust or check it.
2. Crossed, with the trend, not just a single alarming number: two months down is signal, one bad reading might be noise.
3. The pre-agreed response is retrieved and recommended, but enacting it stays the member's decision, per the governing principle.
4. The radar names the severity honestly and does not over-escalate: one handled trip does not trigger a re-plan, which is what keeps the radar trustworthy.

The same shape across audiences: a **professional** watches her function's two real risk metrics and escalates the one crossing with the pre-agreed response named; in **real life**, the radar catches the insurance renewal approaching and the grocery budget line crossing, and proposes the household's agreed response to each.

**Bad example (named failure mode: alarm fatigue and false precision).**

> "12 risks flagged this month: engagement is down a bit, competitors seem active, the market feels uncertain, cash is probably getting tight, we should watch everything closely, growth might be slowing..."

Failure mode: alarm fatigue built on false precision. Twelve vague "risks", none tied to a threshold, none sourced ("probably", "seems", "feels"), and "watch everything" which means watch nothing. A radar like this trains the member to ignore it, so the month a real indicator crosses, the signal is lost in the noise. The skill must refuse this: report only what crossed or is genuinely approaching a defined threshold, source every reading or label it unverified, and let a quiet month be quiet.
