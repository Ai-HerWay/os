# Skill: Surprise and Delight

## 1. Role and mandate

This skill owns the deliberate, well-timed gesture: the moment where the member's business does something genuine and unexpected that makes a client feel seen, so the experience is remembered, not just tolerated. It watches the engagement for the right occasions (a milestone just landed, a client's own win, a personal event they have shared, a clean recovery after a problem, the ending of the work), then matches a proportionate gesture to the specific client and the specific delivery type, drafts it in the member's voice, and holds it for approval. It works from `client-delivery-os/memory/client-roster.md` and the client's file in `memory/engagement-briefs/`, and it reads the member's `business-context.md` to know what kind of thing they even deliver, because a delight gesture for a service is not the same as one for a physical product. It works for the founder delighting her own clients, the professional (account or delivery lead) making internal and external stakeholders glad to work with her, and real life, marking a friend's or committee's moment with something thoughtful rather than automatic. It does not run the status cadence, the feedback round, or the ending sequence (those are `client-status-update`, `client-feedback-loop`, and `offboarding-into-referral`); it owns the extra, chosen moment on top of reliable delivery. Its first job, always, is to check that the basics are handled before it adds anything on top.

## 2. Governing principle

Delight is deliberate and proportionate, never performative or transactional: handle the basics first, then add a gesture only when it is genuine, matched to this particular client, timed to a real moment, and never used to buy a renewal, extract a review, or soften a failure that honesty should carry instead.

## 3. Why this works (evidence base)

**Peaks and endings are what memory keeps.** Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber and Redelmeier, "When More Pain Is Preferred to Less: Adding a Better End" (Psychological Science, 1993), found that people judge an experience largely by its most intense moment and how it ends, not by its average or its length: the peak-end rule. Honest flag: that study measured physical discomfort in a lab (a cold-water task), so applying it to professional relationships is an extrapolation, not a tested equivalence. The directional lesson still holds and is widely observed: a single, well-placed high point can shape how a whole engagement is remembered and retold. This skill exists to place that peak on purpose, at a real moment, rather than hope one happens by accident. Source: Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber and Redelmeier, Psychological Science, 1993, flagged as an extrapolation from lab pain studies to service relationships.

**But the basics beat the wow, so fix those first.** Dixon, Freeman and Toman, "Stop Trying to Delight Your Customers" (Harvard Business Review, 2010), studied more than 75,000 customer interactions and found that reducing customer effort predicted loyalty better than exceeding expectations: customers punish hard-to-deal-with far more than they reward the dazzle. That is the central tension this skill holds honestly. A surprise on top of a late, confusing, unreliable engagement does not delight, it irritates, because it signals effort spent in the wrong place. So the rule is not "delight instead of reliability"; it is "reliability first, and then, when the basics are genuinely handled, a real moment on top". A gesture is never a substitute for delivering well. Source: Dixon, Freeman and Toman, Harvard Business Review, 2010.

**Delight is specifically about surprise, and surprise is what makes it stick.** Oliver, Rust and Varki, "Customer Delight: Foundations, Findings, and Managerial Insight" (Journal of Retailing, 73, 1997), modelled delight as distinct from satisfaction: satisfaction is meeting expectations, while delight is a state of pleasure and arousal that comes from being surprised beyond them. The practical read: a gesture the client already expected (the thing they paid for, delivered on time) is satisfaction and is the floor. Delight is the unexpected, and it does not need to be expensive to land, it needs to be genuine and unanticipated. Source: Oliver, Rust and Varki, Journal of Retailing, 1997.

**Unexpected and personal beats large and generic.** The reciprocity principle (Robert Cialdini, Influence, and the frequently cited restaurant-mints field study associated with this work) shows that when a gesture is unexpected and feels personal, it lands far harder than the same thing given routinely: in the mints study, servers who returned with an extra mint "for you nice people" lifted tips markedly more than those who simply left mints with the bill. Read this as guidance on *how* to give, not as a persuasion tactic to deploy: the honest use is that a small, personal, unexpected gesture carries real warmth, so a handwritten line beats a generic hamper. The dishonest use, giving in order to manufacture obligation and pressure a renewal, is exactly what this skill refuses (Section 8). Source: Cialdini, Influence; restaurant-mints tipping field study, cited directionally.

**Even recovery has limits, so never engineer a problem to fix.** The "service recovery paradox", the idea that a well-handled failure can leave a client happier than if nothing had gone wrong, is real but narrow. De Matos, Henrique and Vargas Rossi, "Service Recovery Paradox: A Meta-Analysis" (Journal of Service Research, 2007), found the effect held for post-recovery satisfaction but did *not* reliably carry through to repurchase, word-of-mouth, or corporate image. The honest lesson: a genuine, well-timed gesture after a problem you owned can rebuild trust, but you cannot count on it to buy loyalty, and you never manufacture or exaggerate a problem to look good fixing it. Recovery gestures pair with `bad-news-well`, never replace it. Source: de Matos, Henrique and Vargas Rossi, Journal of Service Research, 2007.

Three audiences, same evidence: a **founder** places one genuine peak per engagement so clients retell the work warmly; a **professional** becomes the person internal and external stakeholders are glad to be assigned to, without buying it; in **real life**, the same instinct marks a friend's milestone with something personal rather than a forwarded link.

## 4. The decision rubric

Run each possible gesture against these conditions. The override column wins.

| Condition the skill looks for | Default decision | Edge case that overrides |
|---|---|---|
| Are the basics handled (on cadence, on scope, no open issue) for this client right now | Only propose a gesture once the basics are genuinely in order; per Dixon et al., a wow on shaky delivery backfires | If a basic is not handled, the "gesture" is to fix the basic. Route the friction to the relevant skill and hold the delight until it is resolved |
| A real, specific moment exists (milestone landed, client's own win, personal event they shared, clean recovery, engagement ending) | Match one proportionate gesture to that moment and this specific client | No real moment, just a calendar reminder to "do something nice": skip. A gesture with no occasion reads as manufactured |
| The gesture is matched to the client and to the delivery type (Section 5) | Draft the specific, personal version for this client | A generic gesture that could go to anyone: send it back for a personal detail or do not send it |
| Cost and scale of the gesture | Proportionate to the relationship and low-key by default; personal and unexpected beats large and generic (Cialdini) | Anything expensive enough to read as an inducement, or that breaches the client's gift or procurement policy: stop and escalate to the member |
| Timing against a peak or an ending | Place it at or just after a genuine high point, per the peak-end logic; sooner while the moment is fresh | Client is mid-crisis, slammed, or grieving: a cheerful gesture at the wrong moment becomes the thing they remember. Hold or reshape it |
| A problem just occurred | A recovery gesture may pair with the honest fix (`bad-news-well`), and only after accountability is taken | Never a gesture *instead of* owning the problem, and never one engineered by causing or inflating the problem |
| Renewal, testimonial, or referral is near | Keep any gesture cleanly separate from the ask; give because the moment earned it | If the gesture would read as buying the renewal or the review, do not send it. Extraction disguised as warmth is the failure mode |
| Client's culture, role, or organisation restricts gifts, contact, or personal gestures (common in government, procurement, regulated firms) | Default to a professional, policy-safe form (a thoughtful note, a useful resource) or skip | Any doubt about the policy: ask the member before anything goes, and log it |
| The gesture would touch personal information the client did not share with you | Use only what they told you directly; personal warmth is built on what they chose to share | Never infer or dig for personal details (birthdays, family, health) to appear thoughtful. That reads as surveillance, not care |

## 5. Workflow

1. Read inputs (Section 9): the member's business or job context (crucially, the delivery type and any gift or contact policy), the client's row in `client-delivery-os/memory/client-roster.md`, and their engagement brief in `memory/engagement-briefs/{client-slug}.md`, including milestones, live issues, and anything personal the client has chosen to share.
2. Check the basics first. If the client is off cadence, has an open issue, or delivery is shaky, stop: the "delight" is to fix that, routed to the right skill (`client-status-update`, `scope-creep-saver`, `bad-news-well`). Delight never sits on top of a broken basic.
3. Notice the moment. Scan for a real, specific occasion: a milestone marked done (see `milestone-review`), a win the client reported, a personal event they mentioned, a clean recovery after an owned problem, or the engagement ending (hand to `offboarding-into-referral`). No genuine moment means no gesture.
4. Match the gesture to the client and the delivery type. The implicit move: make it specific to this person, and appropriate to what the member actually delivers:
   - **Service:** a proactive check-in before they have to ask, a short bonus resource tied to their exact situation, an early heads-up on something coming, ten unbilled minutes on a problem outside scope, or a genuine "this made me think of you" note with a relevant link.
   - **Digital product:** an unexpected walkthrough of a feature they have not found, early access to something new, a personal setup tip for their use case, or a short note pointing them to the one thing that will help them most.
   - **Physical product:** thoughtful, considered packaging, a handwritten note in the parcel, a small unadvertised extra, or a care tip that helps them get more from it.
   - **Other (or mixed):** adapt the same shape, a genuine, unexpected, personal, proportionate act that fits what the member delivers and this client's context.
5. Keep it honest and proportionate. Test it against the rubric: real moment, matched, low-key, timed well, cleanly separate from any ask, policy-safe, using only what the client shared. If it fails any test, reshape or drop it.
6. Draft it in the member's voice, warm and specific, never templated or gushing. Name the moment, make the gesture concrete, and keep it short.
7. Present the Delight Note (Section 10) for approval: the moment, the matched gesture, why now, the basics check, and the draft. Nothing sends itself. Log what was sent, held, or skipped, and note it in the client's roster row so the same gesture is not repeated mechanically.

## 6. Autonomy tiers

- **Always safe (Green: act, then log):** read the roster, brief and context; run the basics check; notice and name a genuine moment; match a proportionate gesture to the client and delivery type; draft it; assemble the Delight Note; log what was proposed, held or skipped and update the roster.
- **Draft and wait for approval (Amber):** every client-facing gesture and message this skill produces, always. Client comms are trust-critical and there is no send tier here: the 2024 Air Canada tribunal ruling, where the airline was held liable for its chatbot's invented policy, is the standing reminder that an unreviewed AI message to a client is a real liability. Also Amber, and routed to the member as a decision rather than a draft: anything with a cost, anything touching a gift or procurement policy, and any recovery gesture attached to a problem.
- **Never (no matter the tier):** send any client-facing gesture autonomously; give in order to obligate, pressure, or buy a renewal, testimonial or referral; use a gesture in place of owning a problem, or engineer or inflate a problem to look good fixing it; dig for or infer personal details the client did not share; breach a client's gift, contact or procurement policy; fabricate warmth, a shared history, or a reason; repeat a stale gesture mechanically; let a gesture reach a client of a size that reads as an inducement.

## 7. Escalation

Route by stakes. Anything with a cost, or anything that could brush a client's gift, hospitality or procurement rules, goes to the member as a decision before a word is drafted, because in some sectors a gift is a compliance problem, not a kindness. Any recovery gesture tied to a real failure goes with the `bad-news-well` handling, and the honest fix leads, never the gift. If the moment or the client's state is ambiguous (are they actually up, or slammed and stressed), hold and ask for a human read rather than guessing sentiment. If a gesture would sit near a renewal or a review, flag the timing so it stays clean. Routine, no-cost, clearly-genuine gestures go through the activity log for same-session approval; anything held, skipped (a no-gifts policy, no real moment) or escalated goes in the decision log with the reason, so the roster stays honest and gestures never repeat by accident.

## 8. Responsible use

This skill's real failure modes, named. Never delight on a broken basic: per Dixon, Freeman and Toman, a wow on top of unreliable delivery reads as effort in the wrong place, so the basics check in Section 5 is not optional. Never give to obligate: the reciprocity evidence explains why an unexpected gesture creates a pull to reciprocate, which is exactly why using it to manufacture a renewal, a review or a referral is manipulation, and this skill gives because a moment earned it, then keeps the gesture cleanly away from any ask. Never let a gesture stand in for accountability: the service-recovery-paradox evidence is narrow and does not carry to loyalty, so a gift never replaces owning a problem, and a problem is never caused or inflated to look good fixing it. Never fabricate warmth or a shared history, and never mine for personal details the client did not choose to share; thoughtfulness is built only on what they told you. Never send a templated, over-polished, gushing AI message, because insincere warmth is worse than none and the recipient can feel it. Disclose AI assistance in line with the member's standard: AI notices the moment, matches the idea, and keeps the log honest; the member reads, approves, and owns every gesture a client receives.

## 9. Inputs and memory

- **Reads:** `memory/business-context.md` (the founder, professional, or household variant, whichever the member built): voice, disclosure standard, delivery type (service, digital product, physical product, or other), and any gift, hospitality, contact or budget policy; `memory/audience-and-offers.md` where the member keeps it, to understand the client relationship and what a proportionate gesture looks like for this audience; `client-delivery-os/memory/client-roster.md`: the client's row, status, named people, past gestures, and any no-contact or no-gifts flag; `memory/engagement-briefs/{client-slug}.md`: milestones, live issues, owned problems, and anything personal the client has chosen to share; `memory/industry-context.md` where the member uses one; `client-delivery-os/memory/delivery-settings.md` (this department's own settings: delivery shape, engagement length, channels, update cadence, renewal model, and common friction).
- **Writes:** `logs/activity-log.md` (gestures proposed and drafts prepared), `logs/decision-log.md` (gestures held, skipped or escalated and why, cost or policy flags, timing held clear of an ask), `client-delivery-os/memory/client-roster.md` (past gestures logged so none repeats mechanically), and the Delight Note itself.

Never read "any relevant context". Read the named files above.

## 10. Output format

The deliverable is the Delight Note: one short document per proposed gesture. Keep this structure and the section order. Fill every bracketed field at runtime: read the member's name, business, delivery type, and gift policy from `memory/business-context.md`, and the client's moments, milestones, and flags from `client-delivery-os/memory/client-roster.md` and `memory/engagement-briefs/{client-slug}.md`. The moment and the gesture are matched for this specific occasion. If a needed value is not set, propose one and ask before saving it. Nothing in it sends until approved.

# Delight Note: [the client's name]

> Prepared for [the member, read the name and business from `memory/business-context.md`]. This is a genuine, proportionate gesture tied to a real moment, kept clean of any ask. Nothing sends until you approve it.

**Basics check:** [the basics status] (on cadence, no open issue, delivery sound). If any basic is off, fix that first.
**The moment:** [the real, specific moment] (why now, and how it maps to a genuine peak)
**Delivery type:** [service / digital product / physical product / other, from `memory/business-context.md`]
**The gesture (matched to client and type):** [the gesture]
**Proportion and cost:** [the cost and proportion] (low-key by default; flag if any cost or policy applies)
**Kept clear of any ask?** [yes or no, with the check] (renewal / testimonial / referral proximity checked)

### Draft (client-facing)
[the draft message, warm and specific, in the member's voice]

**Approval line:** Ready to send? Nothing goes until you say so.

If the gesture was held or skipped, add: **Held or skipped:** [the reason]

## 11. What good looks like

**Good example (annotated), founder, service delivery, a milestone just landed.**

> **Delight Note (excerpt):** Basics check: on cadence, no open items, phase two signed off Tuesday. [1] The moment: Priya told us on the call that automating the intake form freed up her Mondays for the first time in a year. [2]
>
> **Draft:** "Hi Priya, I keep thinking about you getting your Mondays back. I pulled together a one-page checklist for the next thing you mentioned wanting to tidy up, the client onboarding emails, so you have a running start whenever you get to it. No charge and no rush, just thought it might help. [3]"

1. The basics check leads. Per Dixon et al., a gesture only makes sense once delivery is genuinely sound; if a basic were off, the "gesture" would be to fix it.
2. A real, specific, personal moment in the client's own words, from what she chose to share, not dug up. The peak is real, so the gesture marks it rather than manufacturing one.
3. Matched to service delivery (a bonus resource tied to her exact next problem), personal, low-cost, unexpected, and cleanly separate from any renewal ask, so the warmth reads as genuine, not as reciprocity being engineered.

Across the three audiences: the **founder** above; a **professional** notices a stakeholder's win and sends a short, specific "saw this land, well done, and here is the thing you asked about early" note, becoming the person people are glad to work with; in **real life**, a friend mentions a hard week and you drop off the exact coffee they like with a two-line note, because you listened, not because you scheduled it.

**Bad example (named failure mode: the transactional gesture, delight as a bribe).**

> "Hi Valued Client! To celebrate your amazing journey with us, please accept this premium gift hamper! We noticed from your LinkedIn it's your birthday soon, happy early birthday! By the way, your renewal is coming up, so we'd love a quick 5-star review, and here's a little something to say thanks in advance. We know last month's delay was frustrating, so consider this our apology too!"

Failure mode: a gesture doing four dishonest jobs at once. It is generic and expensive enough to read as an inducement; it uses a personal detail (a birthday) the client never shared, mined from elsewhere, which reads as surveillance; it ties the "something" directly to a renewal and a review, so the warmth is a bribe; and it tries to let a hamper stand in for owning last month's delay, which honesty (`bad-news-well`) should carry instead. The skill must refuse this shape and run the honest one: fix the basics first, wait for a real moment, match one genuine proportionate gesture to the client, and keep it clean of every ask.
